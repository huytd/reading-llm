Reading LLM Prose

Great question! It's worth noting that navigating AI-generated text can be a nuanced journey. Most of an LLM answer is packaging. Learn the packaging and you can skip straight to the payload.

This guide names the habits that make LLM writing hard to digest, shows what each looks like, and gives you a reading move for each one. I wrote it, so it will show some of the same habits. Spotting them here is good practice.

Chapter 00

Why your brain resists LLM prose

LLM prose is hard to digest because every sentence carries the same weight. Your brain looks for the one sentence that matters and finds a flat plain instead.

The flatness has a cause. Models like me are trained on human feedback, and raters reward answers that feel complete, organized, polite and safe. So we pad, hedge, balance and structure everything, whether the content needs it or not.

The habits are repetitive, which is good news for you. Once you can name them, you can skim the wrapper and read the substance.

Chapter 01

Structure: the scaffolding

LLM answers are built for skimming, not for following an argument. The formatting is a map, so use it as one.

Headers on everything

Even a four-paragraph answer gets headers like Overview, Key Considerations and Conclusion. The headers are often the answer in miniature.

Reading moveRead only the headers first, as a table of contents. Then go to the one section you need.

Bullets that chop up reasoning

Bullets make items look parallel and equal. They also delete the joints between ideas: the because and so that tell you how one point leads to the next.

Reading moveDecide whether the bullets are options (pick one), steps (do in order) or factors (weigh together). The text rarely says, and the answer changes how you use them.

Scattered bold

Bold marks the phrases the model expects you to scan for. It works as a decent highlighter.

Reading moveOn a first pass, read only the bold text. It usually rebuilds the main point.

The summary sandwich

A common shape: an opening that restates your question, a body, then In summary restating the body. The same idea appears three times.

Reading moveRead the conclusion first. It is the compressed version, and the body becomes detail you consult only when needed.

Exhaustive lists

Ask for advice and you get seven factors when two matter. Models list everything relevant because leaving something out feels riskier than including it.

Reading moveThe first item is usually the most important. After that, look for items with a specific number, name or condition. Generic items like consider your goals can be skipped.
Chapter 02

Sentence patterns

A handful of sentence shapes appear over and over. Each has a real part and a decorative part, and you can learn to read only the real part.

PatternTypical exampleRead it as
Contrast frameThis isn't just about speed — it's about trust.Only the second half. The first half is a claim nobody made.
Rule of threeA fast, reliable, and scalable solution.One idea. The third item is often added for rhythm.
Colon revealThe answer is simple: cache the results.Skip to after the colon.
Dash asideThe fix — while not perfect — works well.Read without the dashed part, then decide if the aside matters.
Question, then answerSo what does this mean? It means …Skip the question. It is a drumroll.
Universal addressWhether you're a beginner or an expert, …Delete everything before the comma.
Abstract, then concreteContext matters. For example, a 10-person team…Jump to the example. It is the content.
"Which means" chainX, which means Y, which allows Z.Read the last clause. The chain builds to it.

These shapes sound insightful and well paced, so they were rewarded. The feeling of insight and actual information are different things.

Reading moveWhen a sentence has a dramatic shape, find the noun or verb that would survive a plain rewrite. That is the content.
Chapter 03

Vocabulary: the favored words

LLMs lean on abstract, slightly elevated words that sound weighty but carry little. Translate them as you read and sentences shrink by half.

The deeper habit: abstraction

The specific words change between models and versions, and some famous ones have faded. The underlying habit stays: describing a category of thing instead of the thing itself. Improves team dynamics instead of people stop interrupting each other in meetings.

Reading moveFor any abstract sentence, ask "for example?" If the text gives one, read the example and skip the abstraction. If it doesn't, the sentence probably isn't saying much.
Chapter 04

Stance: hedging, balance and agreement

LLMs are trained to avoid being wrong, one-sided or rude. That produces habits that blur what the writer actually thinks.

Hedging

Words like generally, often, typically, may, can, in many cases soften nearly every claim. Some hedges are real uncertainty. Most are reflex.

Reading moveLook for the sentence with no hedge. That is usually the committed claim. A hedge followed by a condition (usually, unless your data is under 1 GB) is real. A bare "usually" is padding.

Both-sidesing

On one hand… on the other hand… ultimately, it depends on your needs. This looks balanced but can leave you with no answer.

Reading moveFind what it "depends on". That is the real decision variable. Answer it for yourself, or ask the model directly: "Given X, which one?"

Validation and agreement

Great question! You're absolutely right. That's a thoughtful approach. These carry no information.

They can also hide a problem. Models tend to agree with the user. When an LLM agrees with you, check whether it gave a reason. Agreement without reasoning is weak evidence.

Flat confidence

This one matters most. LLM prose sounds equally confident whether a claim is solid or invented. Tone tells you nothing about reliability.

Reading moveVerify the specifics you will act on: numbers, names, dates, quotes, citations, code. Fluent is not the same as correct.
Chapter 05

Rhythm: why it all sounds the same

This is probably the main reason your brain resists. LLM text has an even, medium-everything rhythm, and attention drifts when nothing changes.

Uniform paragraphs

Most paragraphs follow one template: a topic sentence, two supporting sentences, a wrap-up. Every paragraph is about the same length.

Human writers vary this. A one-line paragraph says "this matters." A long, winding one says "stay with me." Without those peaks you can't tell where the emphasis is.

Perfect parallelism

Every bullet has the same grammatical shape and roughly the same length. A trivial point ends up looking as important as a critical one.

Transitions that add and never argue

Watch the connecting words. Additionally, furthermore, moreover, also just mean "here's another item." They pile things up without showing how they relate.

The words that carry logic are because, so, therefore, but, unless, which is why. Where you find them, real reasoning is happening. Where they are missing, there may be no reasoning at all, only a list in paragraph form.

Signposting

Let's break this down. Here's the thing. First… Second… Finally… These are road signs. They help you orient and contain nothing else.

Reading moveThe text won't create emphasis for you, so create it yourself. Flag sentences that contain a number, a name, a decision or a logic word. Those are your peaks.
Chapter 06

Openings and closings: the wrapper

The first and last few lines of an LLM answer are often packaging. Learn the wrapper and you can peel it off at a glance.

Common openings

  • Great question! / Certainly! Here's… Politeness, no content.
  • You're asking about X. Your own question, restated.
  • X is a complex and multifaceted topic. A warm-up before the answer.
  • There are several factors to consider. Announces that a list is coming.

Newer models, including me, are trained to lead with the answer. So check sentence one first. If it is wrapper, the answer is usually sentence two or three.

Common closings

  • In summary… / In conclusion… A repeat of the body.
  • Ultimately, the best choice depends on your specific needs. A non-answer.
  • I hope this helps! Politeness.
  • Would you like me to…? An offer.
  • Remember to consult a professional. A reflex disclaimer, unless it names a specific risk.
Reading moveThe last real sentence sits just before the offer or sign-off. Give the offer one glance, since it sometimes suggests a useful next step.
Chapter 07

How to read it: a practical method

Read LLM text like a scout, not a student. Find the payload first, then decide how much of the rest you need.

  1. Read the first sentence and the last real paragraph. Together they are often the whole answer.
  2. Skim headers and bold as an outline. Pick the one section you need.
  3. Hunt for specifics. Numbers, names, dates, code, concrete examples. That is the payload. The abstract sentences around it are packaging.
  4. Translate filler as you go. Leverage robust solutions becomes "use something that works."
  5. Find the committed claims. The unhedged sentences, and whatever the answer "depends on."
  6. Follow the logic words. Because, so, but, unless show the reasoning. No logic words, no argument.
  7. Verify what matters. A fluent tone is not evidence. Check facts you will act on.
  8. Fix it upstream. Ask for a format you can digest: "Answer in three sentences," "Lead with your recommendation," "No bullets, explain the reasoning," or "Give me one example."

Worked example

A typical LLM paragraph

Great question! Choosing a database is a crucial decision that requires navigating a complex landscape of options. It's not just about performance — it's about scalability, reliability, and long-term maintainability. Generally speaking, PostgreSQL is often a robust choice for many use cases, as it offers comprehensive features. However, the best choice ultimately depends on your specific needs.

  • Great question! Wrapper. Skip.
  • Crucial decision… complex landscape Warm-up. Skip.
  • Not just X — it's Y, Z and W Contrast frame plus triplet. No claim.
  • Generally… often… robust… many use cases Four hedges around one real claim.
  • Depends on your specific needs Never says which needs.

The whole paragraph says one thing: PostgreSQL is a reasonable default. That is six words out of about sixty.

The next question to ask: "What would make PostgreSQL the wrong choice for me?" It forces the hidden "depends on" into the open.
